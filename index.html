Theory & Principle

A hollow glass prism is fabricated by cementing three optically flat, uniform rectangular glass plates of equal dimensions at an angle of $60^\circ$ to form an equilateral triangular prism with an enclosed hollow cavity. The cavity is filled with the liquid whose refractive index is to be determined.

Key Question: Why doesn't the refractive index of the glass walls affect the deviation?

Each wall of the hollow prism consists of a glass plate with strictly parallel faces. When a light ray traverses a medium bounded by parallel surfaces, the angle of refraction inside the glass is equal to the angle of incidence at the subsequent glass-liquid interface. Thus, the glass walls introduce only a tiny lateral parallel displacement of the ray, but zero net angular deviation. The entire angular deviation $\delta$ is produced solely by the fluid wedge itself!

General Ray Deviation:

At the first face: $i_1$ is incidence angle, $r_1$ is refraction angle.

At the second face: $r_2$ is incidence angle, $i_2$ is emergence angle.

A = r₁ + r₂

δ = i₁ + i₂ - A

At Minimum Deviation ($D_m$):

The ray travels symmetrically through the liquid parallel to the base:

i₁ = i₂ = i   and   r₁ = r₂ = r = A/2

Dₘ = 2i - A &implies; i = (A + Dₘ)/2

μ = sin[(A + Dₘ)/2] / sin(A/2)

⚠️ Precautions

  • The glass plates of the hollow prism must be optically flat and of uniform thickness.
  • Ensure no air bubbles cling to the inner surfaces when pouring the liquid.
  • The prism must be placed firmly on the prism table so that its refracting edge is vertical and collinear with the axis of rotation.
  • The slit of the collimator should be made as narrow as possible for sharp coincidence with the vertical crosswire.
  • Always record readings of both Verniers ($V_1$ and $V_2$) to eliminate eccentricity error of the circular graduated disc.
